To My Parents, I am a Concept

To my parents, I am a concept. I represent the idea of their son they failed to have. But I am not a concept. I am a person they tried to shove into a son-shaped mold. Customized even before birth.

I’ve seen the paperwork. They checked every detail about my birth parents. Made sure they were attractive. Made sure I was the race and gender they wanted. I was carefully selected.

Day one, my birth certificate changed. Goodbye, Gavin. Hello, Michael. Soon after, I was strapped to a table by a doctor, my genitals mutilated. No regard for my feelings or autonomy, no consideration that I might one day want to be whole. Just making sure I fit the mold.

It continued in childhood. From an early age, I began to get glimpses of what my parents’ concept of a son looked like. I was sensitive, wanting to please. And I was perceptive, quickly learning what characteristics would be judged based on how they spoke about other people.

Their son loves sports. So I played sports.

Their son went to church. So I went to church.

Their son gets good grades. So I got good grades.

Their son doesn’t act effeminate. So I masked my personality.

Their son is thin. So I managed my appearance.

Their son is social. So I clung to friends even at my own expense.

Most of these expectations were not said explicitly, rather inferred by my young mind based on things my parents said about other people. Regardless, I quickly came to understand the concept of the son they were looking for. But I am not that son. The one whose eyes they recognize, whose face looks like theirs. Who understands them inherently. Whose personality they see themselves in. The son they tried to have.

Far be it for me to comment on fertility struggles, but knowing all that my parents went through to have a child of their own makes me feel like a last resort. I’m not the one they wanted, I’m one of the only ones they could get. I can’t imagine what they went through. The loss, grief, and struggle. But is it now my job to heal those wounds by exemplifying what they could’ve had?

I struggle writing this because, in many ways, I am truly grateful for my parents. They say and do the right thing. They try. They’ve worked hard to give me a better life than the one I might have had with my teenage birth parents. They’re good people, beloved by their community. They support me. They want the best for me. But I can’t help the feeling that “best” to them looks very different than it does to me.

I still feel the same pressures I did as a kid. I still hear the judgmental comments made about others, people I call friends, people I wish to be like. And now, when I see them today, they look me up and down scrutinizing all of the ways I’ve deviated from their plan, culminating in a seemingly-mindless comment about my appearance, jewelry, clothing, or life choices, masked as some kind of concern for my health or safety. Maybe it’s some kind of attempt to save me from judgement, not realizing that they themselves inflict it while the rest of the world releases me from it.

I often wish they were more objective. All the time my friends and family tell me, “you have the best parents.” Only I overhear the condescending remarks, only I see the judgmental glances. If they were more objective, I wouldn’t have to wonder if I’m crazy, if my perception is so warped that I’m somehow making all of this up. Just tell me I’m not what you imagined your son to be. That seems like it would sting less than hearing “we love you” or “we’re proud of you” followed by a list of subtle suggestions on how to live my life. At least I wouldn’t be stuck in this current limbo if we could all just acknowledge their failed concept of a son.

Somewhere out there is the woman who birthed me. I know her name. I want to reach out. Maybe she would know me the way my parents wished to know a son of their own.

But I don’t reach out. I can’t take the risk of failing to meet another parent’s concept of a son. Though I am starting to wonder what that version of me would have been like if I had stayed with her. The one who still bears the name she gave to me. Maybe he’s more human than concept. Maybe that’s the real me. At the very least, he’s a clean slate.

What I do know is that I am finally ready to discover who I am outside of my parents’ mold. I’m ready to confront the part of me I hate the most: the prejudice that I’ve picked up from years of being steeped in their judgments. I’m ready to stand my ground and not accommodate at all costs. And I’m ready and excited to leave the concept of Michael behind and spend some time getting to know the real me.

Hello, Gavin.
